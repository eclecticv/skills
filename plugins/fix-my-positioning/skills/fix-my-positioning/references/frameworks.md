# The five scoring frameworks

Each framework scores 0–2.0 in 0.5 increments. Composite score is the sum, range 0–10.

**Non-overlap principle.** Each framework owns a single analytical dimension. Score them independently — do not let a strong showing in one boost or suppress another.

| # | Framework | Dimension scored |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Dunford — Obviously Awesome (5 components) | Competitive differentiation against real alternatives |
| 2 | JTBD + Ulwick ODI overlay | Customer job clarity (functional / emotional / social) |
| 3 | Raskin — Strategic Narrative (5-part) | Narrative arc and stakes |
| 4 | Osterwalder — Value Proposition Canvas | Value-map fit to ranked pains and gains |
| 5 | Challenger Sale | Commercial insight + buying-committee multi-thread |

Composite interpretive bands:

| Range | Band | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 0–3 | broken | Positioning actively hurting GTM; rewrite from scratch |
| 3–5 | weak | Recognizable positioning but missing multiple dimensions |
| 5–7 | competent | Solid baseline; targeted improvements will move the needle |
| 7–8.5 | strong | Category-competitive positioning with identifiable refinement opportunities |
| 8.5–10 | exceptional | Rare; typically only growth-stage category leaders |

## Framework 1: Dunford — Obviously Awesome (0–2)

**Source.** April Dunford, *Obviously Awesome* (Page Two, 2019); follow-up *Sales Pitch* (2023).

**Five components, scored together:**
1. **Competitive alternatives** — what customers would do if you didn't exist (must include status quo / spreadsheets / "do nothing")
2. **Unique attributes** — capabilities only you have
3. **Value and proof** — benefits those attributes deliver, with evidence
4. **Best-fit customers** — those who care most about that value
5. **Market category** — frame of reference that triggers buyer assumptions

Bonus layer: **relevant market trends** that make buying urgent now.

### Scoring rubric

| Score | Criteria |
|---|---|
| **0** | No named competitive alternatives (or only direct competitors, omits status quo). Unique attributes are generic ("easy to use," "AI-powered"). No target segment named, or "for everyone/modern teams." Category is "platform" / "OS" / "suite" without earning it. |
| **0.5** | Names 1–2 direct competitors but ignores status quo. Claims attributes that competitors also claim. Target segment stated at very broad level (e.g., "B2B SaaS companies"). Category label is me-too ("the leading AI platform for X"). |
| **1.0** | Names 3+ real competitive alternatives including status quo. At least one differentiated attribute is defensible and concrete. Target is segment-level but not exclusionary (e.g., "mid-market finance teams"). Category is a recognizable existing category. |
| **1.5** | Alternatives are rigorously mapped across direct/indirect/status quo. 2+ differentiated capabilities tie to a named value pillar. Target is narrow enough to be exclusionary (firmographic + behavioral). Category matches buyer expectations (not inflated). Market trends referenced to add urgency. |
| **2.0** | Five-component model filled without hedging; a buyer could repeat the positioning back in one sentence. Differentiated capabilities are falsifiable and demonstrable. Target segment is so specific that the positioning clearly *excludes* some buyers. Category choice visibly shapes buyer assumptions (price, feature set, comparison set) in the buyer's favor. |

### Diagnostic questions

- What would your best customers use if you didn't exist (include "nothing" and spreadsheets)?
- What can you do that no alternative can?
- Why does that matter to a buyer?
- Who cares most and what makes them identifiable?
- What category makes your value obvious in one read?
- What market trend makes buying now urgent?
- Can you fill each blank without hedging?

### Stage-aware threshold

Startups under ~$200M revenue should rarely attempt to create new categories. If `--stage seed` or `--stage A` and the company is claiming a new category, cap this score at 1.0 and surface as a critique.

## Framework 2: JTBD with ODI overlay (0–2)

**Sources.** Christensen et al., *"Know Your Customers' Jobs to Be Done"* (HBR, Sept 2016); *Competing Against Luck* (2016); Tony Ulwick, *What Customers Want* (2005).

**Core tenets.** Customers "hire" products to make progress on a job that has functional, emotional, and social dimensions. Circumstances matter more than demographics. Jobs are stable; solutions change. Ulwick's ODI adds the Opportunity Algorithm: score outcomes on Importance × Satisfaction; underserved outcomes (high importance, low satisfaction) are where positioning should concentrate.

### Scoring rubric

| Score | Criteria |
|---|---|
| **0** | Positioning never states what job the customer is hiring the product for. Language is feature-centric ("we have dashboards and integrations"). No pain referenced. |
| **0.5** | Vague pain language ("teams struggle with X"). Functional dimension mentioned but no emotional or social layer. No evidence the pain is real or prioritized. |
| **1.0** | A specific functional job is named and tied to product value. At least one emotional or social dimension referenced. Pain severity implied (frequency, cost, frustration). |
| **1.5** | Functional + emotional + social job dimensions are all addressed. The "before state" (what the buyer was doing/using) is explicit. Pain severity is quantified (hours, dollars, lost deals). Implicit Importance × Satisfaction logic — the job being attacked is one buyers rate highly important and currently under-satisfied. |
| **2.0** | Positioning names a specific job, the circumstance in which it arises, the forces driving the switch (push/pull/anxiety/habit), and the functional + emotional + social progress achieved. A buyer reading the homepage thinks: "that's exactly what I'm trying to get done, and nobody else frames it this way." |

### Diagnostic questions

- When the customer hires your product, what specific job are they trying to get done?
- In what circumstance does the job arise?
- What functional, emotional, and social progress are they making?
- What were they using before and why did they "fire" it?
- Is the underlying job high-importance and currently under-satisfied in the market?

## Framework 3: Raskin — Strategic Narrative (0–2)

**Source.** Andy Raskin, *"The Greatest Sales Deck I've Ever Seen"* (Medium, Sept 2016, 2.5M+ views).

**Five narrative beats:**
1. Name a **big, undeniable change in the world** that creates stakes
2. Show there will be **winners and losers**
3. Tease the **Promised Land** (a desirable future state — not your product)
4. Introduce your capabilities as **"magic gifts"** for overcoming obstacles to the Promised Land
5. Provide **evidence** the Promised Land is reachable

### Scoring rubric

| Score | Criteria |
|---|---|
| **0** | Positioning is entirely product-centric. No reference to any macro shift, trend, or change. No enemy/status-quo named. No future state beyond product features. |
| **0.5** | Gestures at a trend ("in today's AI era…") but doesn't tie it to stakes or urgency for the specific buyer. No named enemy. |
| **1.0** | A real market shift is named and the buyer's stakes are articulated. At least one of {enemy, Promised Land, obstacles, proof} is present but the full arc is not. |
| **1.5** | Four of five narrative beats are present and in sequence. The Promised Land is described in customer-outcome language (not product features). At least one customer proof point is surfaced. |
| **2.0** | All five beats are present, in order, with internal logic: the shift makes the enemy's approach obsolete; the Promised Land is the logical response; the obstacles are real; the capabilities function as "magic gifts" solving specific obstacles; evidence (named customer proof) shows others have reached the Promised Land. The narrative works as a standalone 3-minute pitch without any product screenshots. |

### Diagnostic questions

- What undeniable shift in the world creates urgency for your buyer?
- Who or what is the enemy / losing approach?
- Describe the Promised Land without mentioning your product.
- What obstacles stand between buyer and Promised Land?
- Which of your capabilities removes each obstacle?
- What named customer proves the Promised Land is reachable?

## Framework 4: Osterwalder — Value Proposition Canvas (0–2)

**Source.** Osterwalder, Pigneur et al., *Value Proposition Design* (Wiley, 2014).

**Two halves must fit:**
- **Customer Profile** — Jobs (functional / social / emotional), Pains (undesired outcomes, obstacles, risks), Gains (required / expected / desired / unexpected)
- **Value Map** — Products / Services, Pain Relievers, Gain Creators

**Fit** is achieved when the value map addresses the *top-ranked* jobs, pains, and gains on the customer profile — not when it addresses many low-ranked ones.

### Scoring rubric

| Score | Criteria |
|---|---|
| **0** | Value claims are generic adjectives ("faster," "better," "easier") with no object. No specific pain or gain named. Features listed without tying to buyer outcomes. |
| **0.5** | One or two pains named but not ranked; benefits are stated abstractly. No proof; no quantification. |
| **1.0** | 2+ specific pains and 2+ specific gains articulated. Each pain has at least one named pain reliever (feature or capability). Benefit claims are concrete enough to be demonstrable. |
| **1.5** | Pains and gains are ranked by severity/importance. Value map maps cleanly to the top-ranked items (no wasted effort on low-rank gains). At least one gain creator goes beyond "expected" into "desired" or "unexpected" territory. Quantified proof appears (hours saved, % lift, dollars recovered). |
| **2.0** | Canvas passes the fit test: the top 3 pains and top 3 gains each have a dedicated, differentiated pain reliever or gain creator. Quantified outcomes are tied to specific features. A buyer can trace "this feature → relieves this specific pain → produces this measurable gain." Nothing in the value map addresses a pain/gain the customer profile doesn't rank. |

### Diagnostic questions

- What are the top 3 functional, social, and emotional jobs?
- What are the buyer's top 3 pains (ranked by frequency × severity)?
- What are the top 3 gains (required / expected / desired / unexpected)?
- Which specific features relieve which specific pains?
- Which features create which gains?
- Is every high-importance pain/gain addressed?
- Are low-importance items ignored (good) or wastefully addressed (bad)?

## Framework 5: Challenger Sale — commercial insight (0–2)

**Sources.** Dixon & Adamson, *The Challenger Sale* (Portfolio, 2011); CEB/Gartner research on 6,000+ B2B sellers across 90 companies; *The Challenger Customer* (2015).

**Core tenets.** Challengers operate with three behaviors: **Teach** (deliver commercial insight that reframes how the buyer sees their problem), **Tailor** (pitch to each stakeholder's economic drivers), **Take Control** (of pricing, process, and hard conversations). 53% of B2B customer loyalty is driven by the sales experience — more than brand, product, or price combined.

### Scoring rubric

| Score | Criteria |
|---|---|
| **0** | No reframing insight. Positioning simply describes the product or matches category conventions. No differentiated point of view about the buyer's problem. No evidence positioning speaks to more than one stakeholder. |
| **0.5** | A point of view is implied but not made explicit. Messaging addresses one persona only. No economic-buyer language (ROI, TCO, risk). |
| **1.0** | Positioning contains a clear thesis about *how the buyer's world is changing* or *why the common approach fails*. Economic-buyer framing appears somewhere (ROI, payback period, cost of inaction). At least two stakeholder perspectives addressable (user + buyer, or champion + economic buyer). |
| **1.5** | Commercial insight directly attacks a widely-held buyer assumption or category orthodoxy, and the attack leads logically to the startup's strengths. Three-level value articulation: user (ease/speed), champion (career safety), economic buyer (ROI/TCO). Risk-mitigation language present (security, compliance, implementation). |
| **2.0** | Positioning delivers a teachable commercial insight a champion could repeat to the economic buyer without modification. The insight reframes the problem in a way that advantages the startup's unique capabilities. Messaging is multi-threaded for 3+ stakeholder roles (end user, champion, economic buyer, procurement/IT). Cost-of-inaction is quantified. The positioning arms the champion against the default of "no decision." |

### Stage-aware threshold

If `--motion PLG` and `--acv SMB`, soften the Challenger ceiling — bottom-up PLG positioning at SMB ACV does not require multi-stakeholder commercial insight to succeed. Cap critique severity at "informational" rather than "blocking."

If `--motion sales-led` and `--acv enterprise`, hold to the full rubric — Challenger insight is essential.

### Diagnostic questions

- What commercial insight do you deliver that reframes how the buyer sees their problem?
- Does that insight naturally lead to your strengths?
- Can a champion repeat your insight to their CFO/CRO/CIO?
- Does the positioning speak to ≥3 stakeholder roles with role-specific value?
- What's the quantified cost of doing nothing?
- How does your positioning defeat "no decision"?

## How to score during the SCORING stage

For each framework:

1. Read the rubric above for that framework only — do not let other frameworks color the score
2. Quote 1–3 verbatim extracts from the extracted homepage as evidence
3. Identify the weakest specific element (e.g., "no named competitive alternatives" for Dunford)
4. Assign a score in 0.5 increments
5. Write a one-sentence improvement recommendation

Sum the five scores for the composite. Place into the interpretive band.

**Important:** Do not weight the frameworks. The orthogonality argument depends on equal weighting.
