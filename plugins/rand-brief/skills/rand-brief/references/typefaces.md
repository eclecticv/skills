# Rand's typeface vocabulary — faces, pairing logic, modern equivalents

Rand's typographic positions below are sourced from his 1989 *Type Talks* interview (Mario Rampone; full text on paulrand.design) and from the documented record of his work (IBM corporate history; Fonts In Use). Recommendations of modern equivalents are this tool's, not Rand's — never present them as his.

## What Rand actually said about type

- "The trick is to choose a readable and beautiful face like Baskerville, or Caslon, or Garamond." — *Type Talks*, 1989 **[verbatim]**
- He judged very bold Univers far superior to Helvetica (Helvetica's counters close up at bold weights) — *Type Talks*, 1989 **[paraphrase]**
- He called Futura a 1925 classic but "not exactly the most legible face" — *Type Talks*, 1989 **[fragment verbatim]** — he used it for its geometry, with care.
- He approved IBM's adoption of Berthold Bodoni as house face and had its x-height increased and ascenders shortened; he noted Berthold Bodoni is close to Didot or Walbaum — *Type Talks*, 1989 **[paraphrase]**
- His governing principle was appropriateness and the pair of readability and beauty — not novelty, not fashion.

## The vocabulary

Faces documented in Rand's identities and book work, with modern paths:

| Face (his era) | Character | Rand connection | Licensed modern | Free stand-in (Google Fonts) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| City Medium (Trump, 1930) | squared slab, engineered | basis of the 1956 IBM wordmark and the 8-bar logo | Square Slab 711 (Bitstream) | Zilla Slab or Arvo (imperfect — nothing free nails City's squared curves) |
| Futura (Renner, 1927) | geometric sans, pure circles | recurring in his covers and layouts | Futura PT (Adobe Fonts) | Jost |
| Bodoni (Berthold cut) | high-contrast modern serif | IBM house face under his program | Bauer Bodoni | Bodoni Moda, Libre Bodoni |
| Caslon | warm old-style serif | named by Rand as readable-and-beautiful | Adobe Caslon Pro | Libre Caslon Text / Display |
| Baskerville | transitional serif, even color | named by Rand | Baskerville (system on macOS) | Libre Baskerville |
| Garamond | old-style serif, humanist | named by Rand | Adobe Garamond Pro | EB Garamond |
| Univers (Frutiger, 1957) | rational neo-grotesque, huge family | endorsed by Rand at bold weights | Univers Next | Archivo or Inter (stand-ins, not clones) |

## Pairing logic

Rand's identities pair **one voice for the mark** and **one voice for everything else**, chosen by contrast of structure, not by mood boards:

1. **Geometric or squared mark → humanist or modern serif for text.** The mark supplies rigor; the serif supplies warmth and readability (the IBM pattern: engineered slab wordmark, Bodoni house face).
2. **Serif wordmark → rational grotesque for supporting text.** Keep the grotesque at regular weights for captions, navigation, data.
3. Never pair two faces with the same skeleton (Futura + Jost is redundancy, not a pairing).
4. Two families maximum. A third voice, if truly needed, is a weight or optical size of the first two.

## Choosing for the brief

1. Let the **mark's geometry** pick the primary: squared/gridded idea → City-lineage slab or Univers-lineage grotesque; circular/organic idea → Futura-lineage geometry or an old-style serif wordmark.
2. Let the **register of the business** pick the secondary: technical/product businesses read best with the serif as the warm counterweight; editorial/human businesses can invert (serif leads, grotesque supports).
3. In the brief's Typography section always give three tiers: the historical face Rand would recognize, the licensed modern cut, and the free stand-in — with one sentence on why this voice agrees with the mark and the name.
4. Appropriateness beats novelty. If the choice needs a trend to justify it, it fails the durability criterion.
