# Rand's principles — criteria, philosophy, and the verified quote bank

Everything here is sourced. Quotes marked **[verbatim]** may be quoted in the brief with their attribution. Facts marked **[fact]** may be stated. Nothing outside this file may be attributed to Paul Rand.

## The seven criteria (use these verbatim in the brief's table)

From "Logos, Flags, and Escutcheons" (AIGA essay, 1991; full text at paulrand.design):

> "The effectiveness of a good logo depends on: a. distinctiveness b. visibility c. useability d. memorability e. universality f. durability g. timelessness" **[verbatim]**

Score the current identity against all seven. Practical readings:

1. **Distinctiveness** — could this mark belong to a competitor without anyone noticing?
2. **Visibility** — does it register at a glance, in a feed, on a shelf, at distance?
3. **Useability** — does it work across every medium the business actually uses, in one color, reversed, small?
4. **Memorability** — can someone draw it from memory after one exposure?
5. **Universality** — does it read across cultures and contexts, or does it depend on local/insider knowledge?
6. **Durability** — will it survive daily wear: compression, co-branding, bad printing?
7. **Timelessness** — is anything in it dated to the current decade's fashion?

## What a logo is and is not (the core doctrine)

All from the same 1991 essay, all **[verbatim]**:

- "A logo is a flag, a signature, an escutcheon."
- "A logo doesn't sell (directly), it identifies."
- "The role of the logo is to point, to designate—in as simple a manner as possible."
- "A logo derives its meaning from the quality of the thing it symbolizes, not the other way around."
- "What makes the Rolls Royce emblem so distinguished is not its design (which is commonplace), but the quality of the automobile for which it stands."

From the NeXT presentation booklet (1986) **[verbatim]**:

- "Ideally, a logo should explain or suggest the business it symbolizes, but this is rarely possible or even necessary. There is nothing about the IBM symbol, for example, that suggests computers, except what the viewer reads into it."

Consequences for the brief:

- Never claim the proposed mark will "communicate the brand's values." It identifies; the company supplies the meaning over time.
- Never demand the mark depict the product. It needs an *idea*, not an illustration.
- Judge the current mark on the seven criteria, not on taste.

## Play, wit, and the rebus

- "Without play, there would be no Picasso. Without play, there is no experimentation." — *Graphic Wit* (Steven Heller, 1991) **[verbatim]** (do NOT append the circulating third sentence "Experimentation is the quest for answers" — unverified)
- "I use the term play, but I mean coping with the problems of form and content, weighing relationships, establishing priorities." — *Graphic Wit* (1991) **[verbatim]**
- "The notion that the humorous approach to visual communication is undignified or belittling is sheer nonsense." — *Thoughts on Design* (1947) **[verbatim]**
- "I like things that are playful; I like things that are happy; I like things that will make the client smile." — Cooper Union lecture, October 3, 1996 (his last) **[verbatim]**
- The rebus is Rand's signature device **[fact]**: the Eye-Bee-M poster (1981, in SFMOMA's collection) replaces I-B-M with eye, bee, M. The UPS bow-tied package atop the shield is a visual pun on a parcel. When the brand name contains a latent image or pun, that is the first place to look for the idea.

## Craft convictions

- "Visual communications of any kind, whether persuasive or informative, from billboards to birth announcements, should be seen as the embodiment of form and function: the integration of the beautiful and the useful." — *Thoughts on Design* (1947) **[verbatim]**
- "Good design adds value of some kind, gives meaning, and, not incidentally, can be sheer pleasure to behold." — *Design, Form and Chaos* (1993) **[verbatim]**
- "Don't try to be original. Just try to be good." — spoken on film to design students (Preston McClanahan footage, 1990s) **[verbatim]**; Rand was consciously echoing Mies van der Rohe.
- On modernism: "It means integrity; it means honesty; it means the absence of sentimentality and the absence of nostalgia; it means simplicity; it means clarity." — Cooper Union lecture, October 3, 1996 **[verbatim]**

## Portfolio facts (safe to cite, all **[fact]**)

IBM wordmark in City Medium 1956, 8-bar striped IBM 1972, Eye-Bee-M poster 1981; Westinghouse 1960; UPS 1961; ABC 1962; Cummins 1962; Ford proposal 1966 (rejected, unused); Yale University Press 1985; NeXT 1986 (fee: $100,000, as consistently reported by press accounts); IDEO 1991; Morningstar 1991; EF Education First 1993; Enron 1996 (his last major mark). Books: *Thoughts on Design* (1947), *Paul Rand: A Designer's Art* (1985), *Design, Form, and Chaos* (1993), *From Lascaux to Brooklyn* (1996).

## DO NOT USE — popular but unverifiable

Never attribute these to Rand, even though the internet does:

- "Design is the silent ambassador of your brand." — no primary source exists; absent from the estate's own quote collection.
- "Simplicity is not the goal. It is the by-product of a good idea and modest expectations." — no citation found anywhere.
- "Design is so simple, that's why it is so complicated." — no primary source located.
- "Everything is design. Everything!" — the title of his 2015 Museum of the City of New York retrospective; no primary source for the sentence itself. May be referenced only as the retrospective's title.
- Any first-person "I will solve your problem for you…" presented as Rand's own writing — that wording is verified only as **Steve Jobs' recollection** (1993 filmed interview). See `presentation-format.md` for the correct attribution.

## Choosing the closing quote

Pick the verified quote that comments on what the brief actually did: a rebus idea → a play/wit quote; a quiet geometric mark → "A logo doesn't sell (directly), it identifies."; a brief that strips a cluttered identity → the modernism list. Attribute with source and year, e.g.:

> "A logo derives its meaning from the quality of the thing it symbolizes, not the other way around."
> — Paul Rand, "Logos, Flags, and Escutcheons," 1991
