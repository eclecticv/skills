# The presentation format — how Rand argued for a mark

The brief imitates one artifact: the bound booklet Rand presented to NeXT in 1986 (reproduced at paulrand.design; *Design, Form, and Chaos*, 1993, collects seven such presentation portfolios, including NeXT, IDEO, and IBM work). Understand the method, then apply the voice rules.

## The NeXT booklet method **[fact, documented]**

Rand charged NeXT $100,000 (as consistently reported) for one solution, and delivered the reasoning as a printed booklet that walks from problem to inevitability:

1. **Start with the name as given** — the booklet opens by working "NeXT" typographically: case treatments, letterspacing, existing-typeface studies, and why each fails.
2. **Eliminate before proposing** — each rejected path is shown with its reason, so the reader arrives at the solution having already dismissed the alternatives themselves.
3. **Introduce the idea late and simply** — the cube appears only after the ground is prepared: a computer that was literally a black cube, so the mark is a structural fact, not a decoration.
4. **Justify the details in sensory, concrete language.** From the booklet **[verbatim]**: "Poised at an angle of twenty-eight degrees, the black cube—even without color—is equally effective for black and white use." And: "Tipped at a jaunty angle, it brims with the informality, friendliness, and spontaneity of a Christmas seal and the authority of a rubber stamp."
5. **Name the mnemonic.** The lowercase "e" is singled out as "the mnemonic factor" — the small irregularity that makes the mark rememberable, with its bonus readings (education, excellence, e = mc²).
6. **Sign it and stop.** The booklet ends "Paul Rand / Weston, Connecticut Spring 1986." No appendix of alternates.

## The one-solution policy — attribute correctly

The famous wording is **Steve Jobs' recollection**, from his 1993 filmed interview, of what Rand told him:

> "I asked him if he would come up with a few options. And he said, 'No, I will solve your problem for you, and you will pay me. And you don't have to use the solution — if you want options, go talk to other people.'"
> — Steve Jobs, recounting Paul Rand (1993 interview)

If the brief cites this, it must carry exactly that attribution — Jobs recounting Rand, never Rand directly. The account that Rand sat silent while Jobs read the booklet is secondhand anecdote; if used, frame it as "as the story is told."

## Voice rules for the brief

Write every section as if it were a spread in that booklet:

1. **Declaratives, no hedges.** Delete "perhaps," "we suggest," "could," "might consider." A sentence either earns its claim or gets cut.
2. **Short sentences. One idea each.** Most sentences under twelve words. If a sentence carries two clauses, cut it in two. Colons: at most two per brief. Em-dashes: almost never. Never chain construction details into one sentence; give each measurement its own short line. Read a section aloud — if you run out of breath, it is a run-on, and Rand would have cut it.
3. **Droll, never clinical.** Rand justified an angle with a Christmas seal and a rubber stamp. The brief is a letter from a designer, not a spec sheet. Every brief carries at least one everyday, sensory image — and no more than two. Precision supplies the authority; the image supplies the smile.
4. **Reason in front of conclusion.** Each section states the observation first, the consequence second: "The name is one syllable with a doubled vowel. The mark should be equally compressed."
5. **Concrete over abstract.** Prefer physical, checkable language (angles, counts, materials, everyday objects) to strategy language ("elevates," "resonates," "premium feel" — all banned).
6. **Eliminate visibly.** The identity-today section is the elimination step: it shows what the current mark fails to do so the new idea arrives as the obvious answer, not a whim.
7. **Name the mnemonic factor.** Every proposed mark must contain one small irregularity the reader can hold onto — say explicitly what it is.
8. **Arrive once.** The idea is stated exactly once, in one sentence, at the top of The idea. Everything after it is consequence. Never restate it with new adjectives.
9. **End, don't trail.** After the closing quote and the homage line, stop. No next steps, no "further explorations," no upsell.
